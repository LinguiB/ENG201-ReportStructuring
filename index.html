The Research Process: From Topic to Thesis

Before you write ▾
1 · Interdisciplinary areas 2 · Narrow the title 3 · Research question 4 · Find sources 5 · Thesis: 3 focal points
1

Select an interdisciplinary topic

An interdisciplinary topic combines two research areas into one topic. The two areas must have a relationship, so one area helps explain, affect or solve something in the other. They are not studied separately side by side.

Choose a relationship word that links the two areas:

💥 Impact🗂️ Types🧩 Problem & Solution🧗 Challenges⚠️ Risks
2

Narrow the title down

Two areas together are still too broad. Narrow your title by using at least two of these six factors:

🎭1. Genre / StyleFast Fashion · Vegan Diets · Gothic Architecture · Documentary Films
🌍2. RegionEgypt · South Africa · Mexico
👥3. Population / Age GroupMillennials · Mexican Citizens · Retirees
🚻4. GenderFemale · Male
⏳5. Time / EraCOVID-19 · Cold War · WWII · 21st Century
🔎6. Viewpoint / AspectPositive · Negative · Psychological · Physical · Social · Mental
📚 Area 1+ 🔬 Area 2+ 🔗 Relationship+ 🎯 2+ factors= ✅ Narrowed title
🔑

The keyword is RESEARCHABLE. This is a report: you report what published sources say. Your title must be balanced: not too broad and not too narrow. If you cannot find sources on it, it is too narrow. If you find too many unrelated sources, it is too broad.

📢 Too broadFast Fashion
✅ BalancedThe Environmental Impact of Fast Fashion
🔬 Too narrowThe Water Use of One Fast Fashion Brand in a Single Factory in 2023
🧪

Researchable test: Before you choose a title, search for it. Can you find several academic sources (journal articles, books) AND non-academic sources (reports, news, official websites) that cover both of your research areas together? If yes, it is researchable.

🤓 Examples of narrowed-down titles

The Environmental Impact of Fast Fashion
📝 Research areas:👗 Fashion + 🌱 Environmental Science
🔗 Factors:🎭 genre / style + 🔎 viewpoint / aspect
The Benefits of Bilingual Education for Primary School Children
📝 Research areas:🎓 Education + 🗣️ Linguistics
🔗 Factors:👥 population / age group + 🔎 viewpoint / aspect
The Economic Impact of Heritage Tourism in Peru
📝 Research areas:🧳 Tourism + 💰 Economics
🔗 Factors:🎭 genre / style + 🌍 region

✨ More area pairs → titles

🥗 Nutrition + 🤝 SociologyThe Social Challenges of Adopting a Vegan Diet Among Young Adults
📜 History + 📖 LiteratureThe Impact of the Industrial Revolution on Women Writers in Britain
🚜 Agriculture + 🩺 Public HealthThe Health Risks of Pesticide Use Among Farmers in India
⚖️ Law + 💰 EconomicsThe Legal Challenges of the Gig Economy in the European Union
⚠️

Check your title: Does it name two research areas with a clear relationship? Does it include at least two narrowing factors? If not, it is still too broad.

🎯 Practice: Is this title too broad, balanced, or too narrow?

Read each title and choose. Remember: two research areas + a relationship + at least two factors + researchable.

3

Formulate the research question

Turn your narrowed topic into one clear, open question that cannot be answered with yes or no and that your sources can answer.

📌 Model

Research question: What is the environmental impact of fast fashion?

📝

Your research question comes from your narrowed title. A good question is: focused (one issue) · researchable (evidence exists) · open (not yes/no) · neutral (does not already contain the answer).

❌ Yes / No question
Is fast fashion bad for the environment?
A yes/no answer cannot be a report. You only need one word to answer.
✅ Wh- question
What is the environmental impact of fast fashion?
Your sources give the answer, and you report it in three focal points.
🧩

Add a wh- word at the start of the question: What · How · Why · To what extent · In what ways. Never start with Is · Are · Do · Does · Can · Should.

💥 What is the impact of…?for Impact. Example: What is the impact of heritage tourism on the economy of Peru?
🌟 What are the benefits of…?for Benefits. Example: What are the benefits of bilingual education for primary school children?
⚠️ What are the risks of…?for Risks. Example: What are the health risks of pesticide use among farmers in India?
🧗 What are the challenges of…?for Challenges. Example: What are the legal challenges of the gig economy in the European Union?
🗂️ What are the types of…?for Types.
🔧 How does…affect…? / How can…be solved?for Problem & Solution or a process.
🎚️ To what extent…?when you want to discuss the degree of an effect.

🔍 Practice: Is it a yes/no question or a good research question?

Decide quickly. Look at the first word.

🎯 Practice: Choose the best research question for the title

One option is a yes/no question, and one does not match the title.

🛠️ Build a research question

Choose the relationship, then type your topic without the relationship word (e.g. "fast fashion on the environment").

✅ Check your own research question

Type your question. The checker looks at the first word and the form.

4

Find sources: academic and non-academic

Use both kinds of source. Academic sources give you reliable research evidence; non-academic sources give you current facts, real examples and public information.

📚

⚠️

Check every source: Who wrote it? Is the author an expert or a trusted organization? When was it published? Why was it written (to inform, to sell, to persuade)? Record the details immediately and use the Citation Generator below.

5

Reach the thesis statement (3 focal points)

As you read, notice which ideas keep appearing. Group them into three focal points: distinct from each other, parallel in form, and supported by your sources. Then write one sentence that states your aim and names them.

📌 Model

Thesis statement: The aim of this report is to show the environmental impact of fast fashion in terms of water pollution, textile waste, and carbon emissions.

Focal point 1 → II.1Water pollution
Focal point 2 → II.2Textile waste
Focal point 3 → II.3Carbon emissions
💡

Each focal point becomes one heading in the Review of Literature. Your three focal points should explore the relationship between your two research areas from Step 1.

I. Introduction

10 marks ~200–250 words ▾

Building Your Introduction

1

General statement — Open with a broad, true statement that shows why the topic matters to the world.

2

Background information — Give the reader the context they need: key terms, history, or the scale of the issue. Be extensive here.

3

Research question — One clear, focused question your entire report answers.

4

Thesis statement — One sentence that states your aim and names your three focal points. These three points become your literature review headings.

📌 Model

Research question: What are the benefits of introducing electric vehicles to large cities?

Thesis statement: The aim of this report is to show the benefits of electric vehicles in large cities in terms of carbon emissions, air quality and health, and financial savings.

💡

The three focal points in your thesis become the three headings in Section II. Every heading must match exactly one focal point.

📚

II. Review of Literature

20 marks ~1,000–1,100 words ▾

Body Paragraph Structure

Each heading needs at least one body paragraph that brings two or more sources together. Every paragraph follows this five-part anatomy:

1
Topic Sentence
States the focal point of this paragraph. Do not write "This paragraph discusses…" — state your point directly.
2
Source 1
Present evidence from your first source using a reporting verb and an APA in-text citation. Paraphrase or summarize — do not copy.
3
Source 2
Connect to Source 1 with a transition word (Moreover, Similarly, However…) and add the second source's evidence.
4
Synthesis Sentence
Write 1–2 sentences that combine both sources around your own point. Show what they mean together — not just what each one says separately. This is the moment you connect the dots for the reader.
5
Concluding Sentence
Close the idea and link it back to the thesis. Do not introduce new information here.

What Does Synthesizing Sources Mean?

🧩

The Simple Idea

Think of your sources like puzzle pieces. Each one says something useful on its own, but you are the one who puts them together and explains what the full picture looks like. That "putting together" is synthesis.

It is not just reporting what Source A says, then what Source B says. It is showing how they connect, agree, disagree, or build on each other — and what that means for your argument.

Source 1

Its key idea

+
Source 2

Its key idea

+
Your Point

Why it matters

→
Synthesis

One unified paragraph

Synthesis = Sources + Your thinking, woven together in one paragraph

How to write the synthesis sentence (Step 4):

  • Ask yourself: "What do both sources tell me together that neither one says alone?"
  • Use connection phrases like: Both studies suggest that… / While X focuses on…, Y extends this by… / Together, these findings indicate…
  • Keep it short — 1 to 2 sentences. Then write your concluding sentence.
📌 Full Paragraph Model — Music and Mental Health

[1 — Topic sentence] Music has the ability to help people turn sadness into positive thoughts.

[2 — Source 1] Cherry (2019) explains that music helps people manage pain, reduce stress, and improve mood. Cherry (2019) also asserts that music therapy is a safe, low-risk way to reduce anxiety and depression in patients with neurological disorders.

[3 — Source 2] Moreover, Welch et al. (2020) report that adults in their eighties with mild to moderate dementia who join a music and movement programme show better verbal fluency and attention.

[4 — Synthesis] Together, these findings indicate that music's positive effect on the mind extends across different age groups and conditions — from everyday stress to clinical dementia.

[5 — Concluding sentence] Therefore, music clearly supports mental health, especially for patients who struggle with sadness.

Tense Rule

⏱️

Which tense do I use when reporting evidence?

— A quick guide
✅ Use these

Present simple

Cherry (2019) explains that…

Present perfect

Researchers have found that…

❌ Avoid this

Past simple

Cherry (2019) explained that…

Even if the study was published years ago, treat the authors' ideas as still true — so keep it present tense.

💡 Why? Academic writing treats published findings as ongoing knowledge, not past events. Smith's 2005 article still argues, shows, and suggests — it did not stop when it was published.

Reporting Verbs

Instead of always writing "Smith says…", choose a verb that matches what the author is actually doing. Select a function below to see the verbs and examples.

Presenting

Use these when the author is simply sharing or explaining information — no strong opinion involved.

describesnotesmentions outlinespresentsreports showsstatesexplains revealsillustratespoints out
Example sentences

Smith (2021) describes the effects of screen time on sleep patterns in teenagers.

The study reveals a significant gap between rural and urban health outcomes.

Agreeing

Use when the author supports, confirms, or aligns with another idea or finding.

acceptsacknowledgesagrees confirmsrecognises supportspraises
Example sentences

Lee (2020) confirms that regular exercise reduces symptoms of anxiety.

Welch et al. (2020) support this view, arguing that movement-based activities improve cognitive function.

Arguing

Use when the author is making a strong or debated claim — more forceful than just presenting information.

claimsbelievesthinks maintainsholds arguescontendsinsists emphasisesproveswarns
Example sentences

Hassan (2019) argues that the current policy fails to protect low-income communities.

The report warns that without immediate action, air quality will worsen significantly by 2030.

Suggesting

Use when the author is putting forward an idea or possibility — but not stating it as definite fact.

suggestsproposesspeculates recommendsurgesadvocates
Example sentences

Brown (2022) suggests that online learning may be more effective for self-paced students.

The WHO recommends a minimum of 150 minutes of physical activity per week for adults.

Concluding

Use when the author reaches a final result or overall finding after their investigation.

concludesfindsinfers discovers
Example sentences

The study concludes that both diet and exercise play an equal role in managing diabetes.

Park et al. (2021) find that students who use digital tools show improved writing accuracy.

Evaluating

Use when the author examines, measures, or critically assesses something — not just describes it.

analysescomparesconsiders examinesinvestigates critiquesscrutinises
Example sentences

Rivera (2020) examines the long-term effects of social media on self-esteem among teenagers.

Chen (2018) critiques the methodology used in earlier studies for ignoring socioeconomic factors.

Disagreeing

Use when an author challenges, questions, or directly opposes another idea or finding.

questionsdoubtschallenges debates refutesrejectsdisputes contradictscriticises
Example sentences

Nguyen (2023) challenges the assumption that remote work increases productivity for all employees.

Malik (2021) refutes earlier claims, showing that dietary fat alone does not cause heart disease.

Emphasising

Use when the author draws special attention to a point — stressing its importance above other findings.

highlightsstresses underscoresaccentuates
Example sentences

The report highlights the urgent need for investment in renewable energy infrastructure.

Adams (2019) underscores the fact that early childhood nutrition has lifelong consequences.

🔧 Grammar Patterns — How Reporting Verbs Connect to the Rest of the Sentence

+ that
The verb is followed directly by a that-clause (subject + verb).
argues that the policy is flawed claims that results are inconclusive suggests that more research is needed finds that outcomes improve
+ noun
The verb is followed by a noun phrase — no "that".
examines the effects of social media investigates the causes of air pollution discusses the limitations of the study
+ preposition
Some verbs need a specific preposition — learn these as fixed phrases.
defines X as Y compares X to Y contrasts X with Y disagrees with X blames X for Y criticises X for Y
⚠️

Before using a strong verb like warns, refutes, or proves — check it in a learner's dictionary first. These words carry strong meaning and can sound wrong if they do not match what the author actually does.

III. Discussion

20 marks ~400–450 words ▾

The discussion interprets the studies you reviewed. Use only information you have already reported — no new sources here.

III.1 Results (Inferences)

Show what the sources mean together: patterns, causes, and consequences no single source states directly.

  • The results indicate that…
  • The studies demonstrate a link between…
  • The analysis confirms…
  • The data suggest that…

III.2 Limitations

Describe what your reviewed sources cannot tell us — a region, group, or aspect not covered.

  • The generalizability of the results is limited by…
  • Due to the lack of available data, the studies cannot confirm…
  • It is beyond the scope of the reviewed studies to…

III.3 Recommendations

Recommendations come directly from limitations. Use action verbs.

  • Further research is needed to establish…
  • Future studies should take into account…
  • It is recommended to…
  • It is suggested to solve this issue by…
🚫

Leave out: new information not reviewed in Section II, recycled ideas (repetition), and inflated claims your sources do not support.

Student Model

📌 EV Example

[3.1 Inferences] The studies reviewed in this paper have all indicated that a large number of electric vehicles in large cities will generally improve residents' quality of life through lower carbon emissions, better air quality and health standards, and some financial benefits.

[3.2 Limitations] The models in the studies, however, cannot represent these effects in regions where electric vehicles are not readily available. For example, Malmgren (2016) focuses on fuel savings, but the calculations use an idealized model, not the real effects of switching. Moreover, Malmgren (2016) does not consider the average income of buyers or the cost of maintaining batteries. In addition, Heidrich et al. (2017) do not expand on electric vehicles in public transport.

[3.3 Recommendations] Therefore, further research is needed to determine the real-world effect of electric vehicles in regions where the market is still emerging. Also, more research is required on maintenance costs compared with gasoline cars. Finally, the potential of electric vehicles for public use needs to be thoroughly researched.

✅

Notice how each limitation leads to exactly one recommendation. Match them up before you write.

🏁

IV. Conclusion

10 marks ~150–200 words ▾
1

Restate the purpose or aim of the study.

2

Summarize the main ideas of the literature review — one sentence per focal point.

3

Point to further research on this topic.

4

Finish with a final thought or a call for action.

🚫

Do not add new sources or new information in the conclusion.

Starter Phrases

The aim of this report has been to… In summary, the reviewed studies show that… Overall, the evidence suggests that… It is therefore recommended that… Further research is needed in order to… In the future, researchers should… This report has demonstrated that…

Reference List (APA 7)

10 marks ▾

In-Text Citation Rules

📖

1–2 authors: Cherry (2019) explains… / (Cherry, 2019) / Smith and Fry (2020) argue…

3+ authors: Welch et al. (2020) agree… — use et al. from the first citation.

Organization: Makati Medical Center (2019) explains…

Direct quote: add page number — (Hanks, 2004, p. 257)

Reference List Formats

Order alphabetically by first author's surname. Use a hanging indent.

Journal Article
Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (Year). Title of article in sentence case. Journal Name, volume(issue), pages. https://doi.org/xxxx
Book
Author, A. A. (Year). Title of book in sentence case. Publisher.
Web Page / Non-Academic Article
Author or Organization. (Year, Month Day). Title of page. Site Name. URL
YouTube Video
Channel Name. (Year, Month Day). Title of video [Video]. YouTube. URL
🔁

Golden rule: Every source cited in the text must appear in the reference list, and every reference must be cited in the text.
